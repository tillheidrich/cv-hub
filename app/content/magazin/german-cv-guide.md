A German CV (Lebenslauf) is a table: your most recent job first, every entry with month and year, ideally no longer than two pages. A photo is common but optional. Marital status, religion and a closing declaration do not belong in it. What German readers look for, and what applicants from abroad often leave out, are your language levels on the European scale (A1 to C2), a line on your work authorisation and an explanation of foreign grades. This guide goes through each part and says where official sources agree and where they don't.

It is written for people applying to German employers from abroad, many of them engineers and IT specialists. The advice comes from the Federal Employment Agency (Bundesagentur für Arbeit), its service for international applicants (ZAV), the government portal Make it in Germany and the relevant laws. Where these sources contradict each other, you will find both views.

## What a German CV looks like

The Federal Employment Agency and Make it in Germany describe the same basic shape:

- **A table, not prose.** Dates on the left, the position or degree on the right.
- **Reverse chronological.** Your current or last job comes first, in experience and in education.
- **Month and year for every entry.** "03/2021 – 08/2024" rather than "2021–2024".
- **Three to five bullet points per job.** Each one says what you did and, where possible, what came of it.
- **Two pages at most.** The ZAV writes that a CV "should not be longer than two pages", and the agency's German portal says one to at most two pages.
- **Tailored to the job.** The agency recommends adapting the CV to each position, which in practice means reordering skills and bullets so the relevant ones come first.

A clean, simple layout is safer than a creative one. Many German employers receive applications through a portal that pulls the text out of your PDF, and two-column layouts or text inside images can get mixed up on the way. A one-column version of your CV for portals avoids that.

## Photo: common, optional, never required

This is where official advice is least consistent. The Federal Employment Agency's German portal says a photo is "no longer a must". Its English guide for international applicants (issue 01/2026) says a photo "is not mandatory, but is still expected by most employers". Make it in Germany says applicants "are often expected to include a photo", depending on the industry.

Employers should not ask for a photo in a job ad: under the General Equal Treatment Act (AGG), such a request can count as an indication of discrimination, as the Federal Employment Agency's application leaflet points out. The choice is yours. A sensible rule for tech jobs: international companies and start-ups rarely care, traditional German companies often still expect one. If you include a photo, use a recent, professional portrait, not a selfie or a passport scan.

## Personal details: what to include and what to drop

Put at the top: name, city (a full address is optional), phone number, email and, if you have one, a LinkedIn or GitHub link.

Date and place of birth are listed as basic data in the ZAV guide. The German portal treats family details and nationality as voluntary. Many applicants from abroad leave out the date of birth, and that is fine.

Marital status and religion are "not required", according to the ZAV. Your parents' names and occupations are not needed either.

Some details are specific to applying from abroad and do help:

- **Work authorisation or visa status**, for example "EU Blue Card holder", "Opportunity Card (Chancenkarte), valid until 05/2027" or "Eligible for an EU Blue Card". Employers can see at once whether they would need to support a visa application.
- **Availability or notice period**, for example "Available from 03/2027" or "Notice period: 3 months".
- **Location**, if you are already in Germany or willing to relocate: "Bengaluru, relocating to Berlin".

## Language levels on the CEFR scale

Make it in Germany recommends stating language skills with the Common European Framework of Reference (CEFR), for example "English: C1". This matters more for international applicants than for anyone else, because many employers look at your German first.

Write it plainly and honestly:

- German: B1 (Goethe certificate, 2026)
- English: C2
- Hindi: native

If you are learning German, say so with a date: "German: A2, B1 course until 06/2027". An employer can plan with that.

## Grades and degrees from abroad

German readers do not know what a CGPA of 8.4 out of 10 means. Write the original grade and add the German equivalent, calculated with the modified Bavarian formula that German universities use. The details, including why there is no fixed conversion table for Indian grades, are in our article on [showing your CGPA on a German CV](/en/magazine/cgpa-german-cv/).

For the degree itself, two things help:

- **The anabin database** of the Standing Conference of the Ministers of Education (KMK). If your university is listed as "H+" and your degree as equivalent, you can note that on the CV.
- **A Statement of Comparability from the ZAB**, the German Central Office for Foreign Education. It costs 200 euros for the first degree and usually takes up to three months, about two weeks if you state in the application that it is for an EU Blue Card and attach the job offer.

For IT jobs you do not need formal recognition, because IT is not a regulated profession in Germany. For the EU Blue Card and other visas you do need to show that your degree is comparable to a German one, via anabin or the ZAB, unless you apply for the Blue Card as an IT specialist without a degree. Make it in Germany explains both routes.

## Gaps, certificates and references

The Federal Employment Agency advises listing gaps of more than two months openly, with what you did in that time: a course, a move, caring for family, a job search. A gap with an explanation reads better than an unexplained jump.

In Germany you send copies of your degree certificates and, if you have them, letters of reference from previous employers. German employers are used to the Arbeitszeugnis, a written reference every employee is entitled to when leaving a job (§ 109 of the Trade, Commerce and Industry Regulation Act). If you have no equivalent, an experience letter or relieving letter from your previous employer is the closest match. Make it in Germany says documents should be translated into German or English.

## Signature and date

Another point where official sources disagree. The ZAV guide says to finish your CV with the date and your signature. The agency's German portal says a signature is "not strictly necessary". For applications through a portal or by email, many applicants skip it today. If you add it, place, date and your name at the bottom are enough.

## What German readers find unusual

Some elements that are standard in CVs from other countries look odd in Germany. Leave them out:

- **A declaration** such as "I hereby declare that the information above is true." In Germany this is assumed.
- **A career objective** in the first line. Replace it with a short profile of two or three sentences: who you are, what you are good at, what you are looking for.
- **Biodata:** parents' names, gender, religion, caste, height, passport number or national ID numbers.
- **Salary as CTC in lakhs.** If an employer asks for your salary expectations, give an annual gross amount in euros, usually in the cover letter, not in the CV.
- **Long lists of strengths and hobbies.** Soft skills belong in the cover letter with an example, according to the ZAV. Hobbies such as travelling are "rather irrelevant"; volunteering is welcome.

A worked example of a full conversion is in [Indian resume to German CV: what to change](/en/magazine/indian-resume-to-german-cv/).

## English or German?

For many tech jobs in Germany an English CV is fine, especially at international companies and in Berlin start-ups. Traditional companies, the public sector and roles with customer contact often expect German. Our [decision guide on English or German CVs](/en/magazine/english-or-german-cv/) lists the signals in a job ad. The simple rule: answer in the language of the ad.

## The cover letter

The Anschreiben is still part of most applications in Germany. Make it in Germany lists the cover letter, the CV and references as the usual documents. Keep it to one page: why this company, what you have done that fits, when you can start and, for applicants from abroad, which visa route applies. German letters follow the DIN 5008 layout: sender, recipient address, place and date on the right, a bold subject line, salutation, text, closing.

## Before you send

- Two pages at most, newest first, month and year everywhere.
- Language levels on the CEFR scale.
- Work authorisation and availability in the personal details.
- Foreign grades with the German equivalent.
- No declaration, no biodata, no CTC.
- A native speaker has proofread it, as the ZAV recommends.
- The PDF has a real text layer: you can select and copy the text.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should a German CV be?

At most two pages, according to the Federal Employment Agency's guide for international applicants. Its German portal says one to at most two A4 pages.

### Do I need a photo on my CV in Germany?

No. A photo is common and many traditional employers still expect one, but it is optional, and under the General Equal Treatment Act employers should not ask for one in a job ad. In international tech companies a CV without a photo is normal.

### Should I include my date of birth and marital status?

Marital status and religion are not required. Date of birth is optional in practice; official German sources list it, but many applicants from abroad leave it out without any disadvantage.

### Is a Europass CV acceptable in Germany?

Yes. The Federal Employment Agency calls it "a solid basis", but notes that many employers prefer a more individual CV. Use Europass as a starting point rather than sending its default layout.

### Do I have to sign my CV?

Not necessarily. Official German sources disagree: one recommends place, date and signature at the end, another says a signature is not strictly necessary. For applications by email or portal, place, date and your name are enough.
